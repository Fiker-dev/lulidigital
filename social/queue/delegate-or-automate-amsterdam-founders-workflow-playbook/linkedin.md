Automation does not remove accountability. It relocates it.

Most founders sort tasks by the wrong question: what can a machine do? The sharper question is who owns the output when it fails — because a machine can run the task, but it can never own the outcome.

Air Canada learned this in a tribunal. When its support chatbot invented a bereavement refund policy, the airline argued the bot was a separate entity responsible for its own words. The tribunal disagreed: a company owns everything on its site, static page or chatbot alike. The system ran the task. The business owned the result, and paid for it.

That is the real line between delegate and automate. Clear input, predictable output, nothing relational at stake — automate it, and stop touching it. Judgment, exceptions, anything published under your name — keep a person in the seat, even when AI drafts the first version. Not because the machine is incapable, but because someone accountable has to be able to catch the one time it is confidently wrong.

The teams that get burned are rarely the ones who automated too little. They are the ones who automated the accountability away along with the task, then found out three days later that nobody was watching.

Before you hand a workflow to a system, answer one question first: when this breaks quietly, who notices?
