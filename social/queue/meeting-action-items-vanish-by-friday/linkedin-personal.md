Every good meeting ends the same way: a clear list of who's doing what. Six items, four of them yours. You close the laptop feeling like a problem got handled.

Then the week happens. A client emergency eats Tuesday. Two of those items only ever lived in your head, and by Thursday your head has moved on. One got written down — in a Slack thread that scrolled away by lunch. The following week the same group meets and quietly re-raises half the list as if for the first time. Because that's basically what it is.

Nobody dropped the ball. The ball was never really caught.

The meeting usually isn't the problem. The decisions were good. What breaks is the twelve inches between agreeing to do something and it becoming a thing that actually gets done. On a big team, someone owns that gap — a chief of staff, an ops lead who writes it all down and chases it up. On a lean team, that person is you, in the ninety seconds between calls, running on memory and goodwill.

Memory and goodwill are wonderful. They're also the least reliable follow-through system ever built.

Here's the part worth knowing: that boring middle step is exactly what AI is good at now. It can sit in on the call, pull out what was actually agreed, and draft it as real tasks — owner, date, one place — in seconds. You spend thirty seconds fixing an owner and approving the list. You're editing, never transcribing. The judgment stays yours; the remembering stops being your job.

Pick your leakiest recurring meeting and try it for two cycles. The shift is obvious fast — and the next meeting starts from the list instead of from scratch.
