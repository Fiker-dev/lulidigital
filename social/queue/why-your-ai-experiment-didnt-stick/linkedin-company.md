Ninety-five percent of company AI pilots delivered no measurable return last year. That was MIT's finding across more than 300 real deployments — and the reason wasn't the models.

The tools were fine. What was missing was the unglamorous part nobody puts in a demo: wiring the tool into the actual work, giving it an owner, and letting it earn its place one real task at a time.

A demo runs on a clean, hand-picked example. An operation runs on the messy version — the request that contradicts yesterday's, the file in the wrong format, the edge case no one planned for. Most tools get judged on the demo and quietly killed by the duty.

The businesses that cross over do one thing differently. They stop asking "is this tool good?" — almost all of them are now — and start asking "which specific, repeating job does this own, end to end, without me babysitting it?"

Get that right on one small task and the AI disappears into the background of your operations, which is exactly where it belongs. Get it wrong and you've bought another impressive demo that dies on a normal Tuesday.

That gap — workflow, not technology — is the whole game. It's also the part that's easy to skip and impossible to fake.
