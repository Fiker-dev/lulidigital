Most AI tools I've watched a founder try ended up as a browser tab nobody opens.

Not because the tool was bad. The demo was genuinely great — it did in seconds what usually eats an afternoon. Everyone nodded. A month later, the old manual habit had quietly filled the space back in.

There's real data behind this, not just a hunch. MIT looked at more than 300 company AI rollouts last year and found 95% delivered no measurable return. The models weren't the problem. The tools never got wired into how the work actually happens.

That's the part nobody demos. A demo is your best example, phrased perfectly. Real work is the client who explains it sideways and the attachment in the wrong format. The demo shines; the normal-Tuesday version quietly dies.

Here's how you can actually use this. Don't go buy a better tool — run the next experiment differently:

- Pick one task you'd be glad to never do again
- Hand it the messy 80%, keep the final 20% and the sign-off
- Put one person in charge and one number on it for two weeks

At the end, ask one question: did the number move? If yes, keep it and add the next task. If no, fix the process, not the tool.

That's the whole difference. The 5% who get real value out of AI aren't the ones with the biggest budgets — they're the ones boringly disciplined about giving it one clear job at a time.

If you've watched a tool fade, the missing piece was never a better model. It was a system built around the one you already had.
